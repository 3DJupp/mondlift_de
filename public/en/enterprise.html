Enterprise

A wheel with twenty gondolas swinging freely from its rim, which tilts from horizontal to vertical during the ride and carries its riders upside down, with no lap bar and no belt. The Enterprise is one of the best-known designs at German funfairs, and the Mondlift is the last travelling one in Germany.

Where the design comes from

It was first built by Anton Schwarzkopf: the first ride was made in 1972, and its premiere was in August 1973 at a funfair in Bad Kreuznach. In the 1970s and 1980s the design became one of the most popular inverting rides at German funfairs.

Huss of Bremen brought out its own version in 1975, revised technically and visually and with a higher capacity. According to the English Wikipedia, the patent on the design was held by Huss, even though the original construction did not come from there. A licence agreement between the two companies is not documented in the publicly available sources. It was the Huss version that caught on internationally and is still being built today.

The story of the Bremen side features a third name: Helmut Kastner, a police officer and model maker who had patented plans for a “flying roundabout” and for a long time found no manufacturer, being turned down by Schwarzkopf among others. That his plans were the basis of the Enterprise cannot be shown from the available sources, however; going by the description, it was a different ride concept. The story is told here because it is often told, not because it has been settled.

How it works

The ride has two phases, and their order is not optional.

  1. Roundabout. The wheel lies almost flat and the gondolas hang down. The drive brings it up to speed: the German Wikipedia gives 14 to 17 revolutions per minute, and up to 14 are quoted for the Mondlift.
  2. Loop. A hydraulic cylinder raises the boom until the axis of rotation is tilted by just under ninety degrees; sources say “not quite 90°” and 87 degrees. The roundabout turns into a loop, and the gondolas pass over the highest point.

The rider is held in place by acceleration alone. The gondolas are mesh cages whose door is locked for the ride; there are no restraints such as lap bars or belts. For the g-force involved, the English Wikipedia gives a value of 3, a figure from a single fact sheet that need not apply to every ride.

Why the gondolas sit right by themselves

The gondolas hang movably but on springs from the wheel rim, and nothing on them is driven. They line up with the resultant force of gravity and centrifugal force: standing still and at low speed, gravity wins and they hang down. As the speed increases, the sum moves outwards and the gondola turns radially. On the raised wheel it therefore always points away from the centre, which at the top means upwards, and that is exactly why the rider stays in the seat.

It is also why the animation on the home page of this site knows no special cases: it computes the sum of the two forces and aligns every gondola with it. The right picture follows by itself, in every phase of the ride.

The manufacturer

Huss was founded in Bremen in 1919 and first made new and spare parts for ship engines. Rides were added in 1969. In 1981 Huss bought the American roller coaster builder Arrow Development; the joint company was called Arrow Huss, and the American part became independent again in 1986 as Arrow Dynamics. On 1 August 2006 the Bremen local court opened preliminary insolvency proceedings; the company became today's Huss Park Attractions GmbH, backed by a group of investors and the Hungarian plant Huss Gépgyár. Manufacturing now takes place in Budapest.

More than thirty-five ride designs go back to the company, among them Break Dance, Ranger, Top Spin, Frisbee and Shot ’n’ Drop. The Enterprise is still in the range:

  • Enterprise 2G: the current basic version.
  • Enterprise 2GH: a mix of hanging seats and classic gondolas.
  • Enterprise 2GH-Plus: the larger version.
  • Giant Enterprise: the big variant with fifteen cars of two seats each; the Orbiter at Canada’s Wonderland is one of them, with a stated 1,200 riders per hour and a ride time of 1:53.

When the design was relaunched, the manufacturer itself spoke of more than seventy-five sold. hussrides.com is the company's website.

How many there are, and why the figures contradict each other

For such a well-known design, the number built is surprisingly unclear. The figures as they appear in the sources:

Figure Number Source
Huss, rides built 74 DeWiki
Huss, deliveries about 50 Wikipedia mirror
Huss, rides sold over 75 manufacturer, at the relaunch
all manufacturers, installations about 64 Wikipedia (English)
Schwarzkopf, rides built over 20 Wikipedia

None of these figures could be checked against a primary source, and some measure different things: built, delivered, sold and installed are four figures, not one. If you want to quote a single number, say which one you mean.

Where the design stands today

As a travelling ride, only the Mondlift is still on the road in Germany. The design keeps running as a fixed installation under various names. The databases list, among others, the Enterprise at Slagharen (given as 21 gondolas, 20 metres ride height and 800 riders per hour), the Kwal at Drievliet, G-Force at Walibi Holland, the Enterprise at Alton Towers with twenty gondolas and the Orbiter at Canada’s Wonderland. In Germany there is one at Freizeit-Land Geiselwind.
